Build Capability, Not Dependency

Build a technology stack that serves your work—not the other way around.

Frontier AI platforms can be extraordinarily useful. They are not the only way to use AI, and no single provider should be treated as the automatic answer to every problem.

During the Circle, you will learn how to think in capabilities before products and compare different ways of assembling an AI-supported system. These may include: commercial frontier models; workplace and enterprise AI; specialist AI applications; open-source and open-weight models; local or privately hosted model options; retrieval from trusted internal information; conventional automation that does not require generative AI; and hybrid systems that use different tools for different parts of the work.

You will not be taught that one category is always safer, more ethical or more intelligent.

You will learn how to make an informed choice.

Ethical does not describe a brand. It describes the decisions around the system.

An ethical AI stack asks:

Purpose

What human or business need is the system intended to serve?

Data

What information enters the system, where is it processed and what must never be exposed?

Capability

Does the selected tool have the right capability for the task, or are we using AI because it is available?

Reliability

How will claims, calculations, citations and decisions be tested?

Risk

What could happen if the output is incomplete, biased, insecure or wrong?

Accountability

Who owns the decision and the consequences?

Human judgment

Which decisions must remain with a person, regardless of what the model recommends?

Portability

Can the process survive a price increase, policy change, outage or decision to change provider?

Your Ethical AI Stack Map

You will leave with a visual map that identifies: the work you are trying to accomplish; the capabilities required; the tools or models being considered; the information entering each stage; privacy and confidentiality boundaries; verification gates; human decision rights; the person accountable for final use; and an alternative route if a provider, product or model is no longer suitable.

The goal is not technological isolation. It is technological agency.
The Human Value of Learning in Community

Shared purpose gets us into the room. Different perspectives make the room valuable.

People do not approach AI—or change—in the same way.

A founder, artist, educator, executive, student, elder, technologist and community builder may enter the same room carrying very different experiences of work, authority, creativity, risk and technological change.

One person may be excited by every new model. Another may be concerned about accuracy, privacy, environmental cost, employment or the effect on human creativity. One person may move quickly and learn through experimentation. Another may need evidence, context and time before placing trust in a new system. One person may see an opportunity. Another may see a risk the first person has missed.

The Circle does not try to turn everyone into the same kind of AI user.

It creates a structured place where people can explain what they see, listen to what someone else sees and examine the assumptions influencing both positions.

Empathy is often easier when another person’s experience resembles our own. Compassion becomes more demanding when someone responds in a way we would not, holds a view we do not share or experiences a concern we do not immediately understand.

The Circle gives participants opportunities to practise: asking before assuming; distinguishing intent from impact; remaining curious when a reaction does not make sense to them; hearing skepticism without treating it as resistance; challenging an idea without diminishing the person; and maintaining boundaries and accountability without withdrawing dignity.

Agreement is not required.

The aim is to become more able to work thoughtfully with people whose experiences, generations, disciplines and perspectives differ from our own. That practice can travel beyond the Circle—into teams, studios, classrooms, client relationships, leadership decisions, families and communities.

Our Code of Conduct

Our Three Agreements for a Safe & Brave Learning Experience

A safe space is not a silent space, and a brave space is not a careless one.

The Circle is designed to empower every member—across generations, professions, creative practices and levels of AI experience—to contribute, question, create and learn. These three agreements give us a shared way to protect the room while still allowing honest difference, thoughtful challenge and experimentation.

They apply to participants, facilitators and guests.

HUMBLE

Listening Up

Humility begins with making enough room to hear another person before deciding what they mean.

  • Mute your microphone when you are not speaking.
  • Listen carefully before responding.
  • Let people complete their thought.
  • Show that you are present by nodding, using reactions, contributing thoughtfully in the chat and engaging with polls.
  • Notice when you have been speaking often and create room for another voice.
  • Remember that expertise is distributed. No one person, profession or generation holds the whole picture.

Practice prompt: What might this person be able to see from where they are standing that I cannot yet see from where I am?

KIND

Pause and Reflect

Kindness is not avoiding difficult conversations. It is choosing not to make another person carry the unprocessed force of our first reaction.

  • Remember that people interpret technology, change and risk through different experiences.
  • Pause before you speak, post or respond.
  • Share your second thought, not your first.
  • Challenge the idea without diminishing the person.
  • Name disagreement clearly and respectfully.
  • Consider both intention and impact.

Practice prompt: Is what I am about to say likely to create clarity, movement or unnecessary heat?

WISE

Assume Positive Intent—and Seek Clarity About Impact

Wisdom begins with curiosity before criticism.

  • Let others finish speaking before you respond.
  • Use the chat to hold a question rather than interrupting.
  • Speak to people kindly and respectfully.
  • Ask for clarity before assigning motive.
  • Be curious first, then critical.
  • If words feel confusing, dismissive or offensive, describe what you heard or felt and ask what the person intended you to understand.

Suggested language: “I heard or felt [name the words or impact]. Can you clarify what you wanted me to understand?”

Assuming positive intent does not require anyone to ignore harmful impact. It asks us to seek understanding before reaching a conclusion, while still making room for accountability, boundaries and repair.

What these agreements make possible

Together, HUMBLE, KIND and WISE help create a room where: a beginner can ask the question everyone else was afraid to ask; a creative can share an unfinished idea without having to prove it is commercially ready; an experienced leader can admit that a new technology is unfamiliar; someone early in their career or creative practice can contribute without being expected to defer; someone with decades of experience can question speed without being labeled resistant; people can disagree without turning difference into disrespect; and everyone remains responsible for the effect of how they participate.

Every Session Includes Practice

You will not spend eight weeks watching someone else use AI.

Every two-hour session includes:

  1. Check-in and pulse poll. See what is changing across the cohort and surface the questions, emotions, creative sparks and obstacles that matter that week.
  2. Plain-language teaching. Understand one capability, limitation or decision without unnecessary technical language.
  3. Live working demonstration. Watch the method applied to genuine business, creative, professional or community work rather than a polished fictional example.
  4. Individual work sprint. Apply the learning immediately to your project or workflow.
  5. Peer exchange. Share what happened, receive feedback and learn how people from other industries are applying the same capability.
  6. Verification. Test the output, identify what influenced it and decide what still requires human judgment.
  7. One clear commitment. Leave knowing what you will complete before the next session and who will help keep you accountable.

Teaching and demonstration segments are recorded. Peer-sharing segments are not recorded, protecting the confidentiality and social safety required for honest practice, creative risk and intergenerational exchange.

Participants also receive a concise recap, the tools used in the session and the next practice commitment.

The Eight-Week Journey

The programme follows the SUMMS™ arc: See, Understand, Make, Manage and Sustain.

You will not memorize a framework and then return to business as usual. You will live inside the method while building something real.

Week 1 · SEE

Your work, your unfinished project and your relationship with AI

Take honest stock of where AI already touches your work, where your time goes and what you feel about the technology. Select the unfinished project or workflow you will move forward during the programme. Feelings are treated as data about the change—not as weakness.

You will produce: a baseline diagnostic; a priority-work map; a defined project or workflow; and a clear success measure.

Week 2 · UNDERSTAND

The AI ecosystem beyond the brand names

Understand what generative AI is and is not. Learn the difference between models, interfaces, search, retrieval, automation, assistants and agents. Explore frontier, workplace, specialist, open-weight, local and hybrid approaches so you can begin matching capability to problem instead of chasing products.

You will produce: a capability map; a current-tool inventory; an unnecessary-subscription check; and an initial set of technology options.

Week 3 · SCRIPT

Stop prompting. Start briefing.

Learn the full SCRIPT™ method: Situation, Clear Intent, Role, Instructions, Parameters and Test. A prompt gives a task. A brief gives direction. You will create reusable briefs that preserve context, clarify what good looks like and require the output to be tested against your actual intent.

You will produce: your first reusable SCRIPT™ brief; a quality definition; and a test plan.

Week 4 · APPLY

Role-specific use cases and a real deliverable

Apply SCRIPT™ to a current responsibility. Produce a real deliverable rather than an exercise. Use the cohort’s different industries as a source of cross-pollination—see how one capability adapts across roles without copying a solution that does not fit your context.

You will produce: a working deliverable; an improved brief; and a role-specific use case.

Week 5 · VERIFY

Accuracy, hallucinations and the limits of confidence

Build the judgment layer. Learn how to identify factual claims, check sources, compare outputs, test calculations, recognize fabricated citations and respond when the model cannot provide sufficient evidence. Examine the difference between a fluent answer and a reliable answer.

You will produce: a verification checklist; a source-checking process; escalation rules; and “do not use AI” boundaries.

Week 6 · BUILD

Turn a one-off output into a repeatable workflow

Move from a useful conversation to a documented process. Map the inputs, steps, decisions, handoffs, human reviews and final output. Test the workflow with more than one example so that it does not depend on a single successful attempt.

You will produce: one documented end-to-end workflow; a workflow diagram; a human-review gate; and a failure-and-recovery plan.

Week 7 · CHOOSE AND ORCHESTRATE

Build your Ethical AI Stack Map

Compare the technology options available for each part of the workflow. Decide what can be handled by a frontier model, workplace platform, specialist tool, open-weight or local model, retrieval system or conventional automation. Consider privacy, accuracy, cost, security, maintenance, portability and human accountability.

You will produce: your Ethical AI Stack Map; a model-and-tool selection rationale; data boundaries; and a provider-portability plan.

Week 8 · SUSTAIN

Showcase, learn from feedback and make the practice durable

Demonstrate what you built, what failed, what you changed and where human judgment remains essential. Receive feedback from the cohort and guest facilitators. Complete your 30-day plan and define how you will continue learning without returning to random prompting or constant product chasing.

You will produce: a final workflow or project demonstration; a personal before-and-after review; a 30-day implementation plan; and your next accountability commitment.

Questions, answered honestly

Do I need to be technical?

No. The programme is designed for nontechnical participants, including professionals, creatives and community builders. It explains capabilities in plain language before discussing products or system design.

If you can describe what you need from a colleague, assess whether work meets your standards and make decisions about your own responsibilities, you already have important foundations.

Is the Circle only for corporate professionals?

No. The Circle is also designed for artists, writers, designers, filmmakers, performers, producers, educators, students, elders, community builders and other creatives who want to turn ideas into responsible, usable work.

It is intentionally intergenerational. Different generations have lived through different technologies, institutions and transitions. The Circle treats that range as a source of knowledge rather than a hierarchy of who is most current or most experienced.

Do I need an unfinished AI project?

You do not need a fully defined project, but you should bring real work.

Participants who already have an unfinished app, workflow, service, process or knowledge product can use the Circle to make meaningful progress on it. Participants without a named project will identify a priority workflow during Week 1.

Which AI tools do I need?

No new paid subscription is required to begin. You can work with AI access you already have, including workplace Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or another available platform.

The Circle teaches capabilities before brands and helps you decide whether a new tool is genuinely necessary.

Will I learn about open-source, open-weight or local AI?

Yes. You will learn the practical distinctions between frontier, workplace, specialist, open-weight, local and hybrid approaches. You will also learn why no category is automatically ethical, secure or accurate.

The purpose is to help you ask better questions, compare options and make responsible choices—not to turn the programme into a model-deployment engineering course.

Will I build a complete technical product?

That depends on the scale of the project and your existing technical capability.

The programme helps you define, structure, test and move forward a real AI-supported project. Some participants may complete a lightweight app or workflow. Others may leave with a validated prototype, stack map, operating process or clearer technical brief.

The programme does not promise to replace software engineers where engineering expertise is required.

What if I am anxious or skeptical about AI?

You do not have to perform enthusiasm. Concern about accuracy, privacy, employment, environmental impact, bias, creative ownership or overdependence can be useful information.

The Circle creates room to examine those concerns and make informed decisions.

What makes the space safe?

Safety is supported through a small cohort, confidentiality expectations, equal opportunities to contribute, protected peer-sharing segments that are not recorded and the Three Agreements: HUMBLE, KIND and WISE.

HUMBLE asks us to practise listening up. KIND asks us to pause and reflect. WISE asks us to assume positive intent while seeking clarity about impact.

Safety does not mean the absence of challenge. It means people can question an idea, admit uncertainty, share unfinished creative work and test new possibilities without unnecessary humiliation or dismissal.

Is this prompt engineering?

No. Prompt engineering focuses on instructions sent to tools.

A prompt gives a task. A brief gives direction.
